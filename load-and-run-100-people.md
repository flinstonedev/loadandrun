# 100 people and the ideas they never got to finish

Seed list for the Load and Run catalog. Each line: who, the idea and its year, what happened, and a status. An asterisk marks people already in the prototype catalog. Status vocabulary: **not picked up**, **partly absorbed**, **actively pursued**, **built and abandoned**, **built late**.

Dates and attributions are from memory and should be checked against the primary source before an entry goes live; a few of the older ones (Llull, Peirce, Goldberg) are known mostly through secondary scholarship.

## Knowledge, documents and information

1. **Ramon Llull** — *Ars Magna* (c. 1305). Rotating paper wheels combining basic concepts to generate all possible arguments; the first design for a reasoning machine. Never mechanized. Not picked up.
2. **Gottfried Leibniz** — *Characteristica universalis* and *calculus ratiocinator* (1679). A universal symbolic language so that disputes could be settled by calculation ("let us compute"). Never built; logic and programming languages are partial descendants. Partly absorbed.
3. **Wilhelm Ostwald** — *Die Brücke* (1911). An institute to standardize and organize all of the world's knowledge; its one lasting output was standardized paper sizes. Institute collapsed in 1913. Partly absorbed.
4. **Paul Otlet*** — Mundaneum and the *réseau* (1934). Universal index queried from home screens over wire. Destroyed in the war. Partly absorbed.
5. **Emanuel Goldberg** — Statistical Machine (patented 1931). Microfilm document search using photocells and coded index marks; predates the Memex by 14 years. Prototype only; Zeiss Ikon never commercialized it. Not picked up.
6. **H.G. Wells** — World Brain (1936). A permanent, continuously revised world encyclopedia as an organ of collective intelligence. Never organized. Partly absorbed (Wikipedia).
7. **Otto Neurath*** — ISOTYPE (1925) and the *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science* (1938). The picture language survived as inspiration; the encyclopedia was abandoned unfinished after twenty monographs. Not picked up.
8. **Watson Davis** — Bibliofilm service and a national documentation institute (1935). Microfilm delivery of any scientific article on demand. Ran briefly at the USDA library, then faded. Built and abandoned.
9. **Vannevar Bush*** — Trails (1945). Curated, shareable paths through documents. Hypertext took the link and left the trail. Not picked up.
10. **S.R. Ranganathan*** — Faceted (Colon) classification (1933). Too complex to apply by hand. Partly absorbed (faceted search).
11. **Manfred Kochen*** — WISE (1972). A synthesizing world encyclopedia, not a collecting one. Actively pursued (AI search), without the governance.
12. **Douglas Engelbart*** — Open Hyperdocument System, viewspecs, the NLS Journal, the ABC model, Dynamic Knowledge Repositories (1962–1992). The hardware was copied; the process ideas were not. Partly absorbed.
13. **Ted Nelson*** — Xanadu (1960) and ZigZag (1996). Transclusion, two-way links, author payment; a multidimensional data structure. Never shipped. Partly absorbed.
14. **Andries van Dam** — FRESS (1969) and Intermedia (1985). Bidirectional links, per-user annotation layers, and links as first-class objects a decade before the web. Intermedia died when Apple dropped the A/UX platform. Built and abandoned.
15. **Wendy Hall*** — Microcosm (1989). Links stored separately from documents in shared linkbases. Lost to the web's embedded links. Not picked up.
16. **Frank Halasz*** — Seven Issues for hypertext (1988). Computed structures, versioning, tailorability remain unsolved. Partly absorbed.
17. **Cathy Marshall** — VIKI (1994). Spatial hypertext: the system infers structure from how you arrange things on a canvas, without asking you to declare links. Research prototype only; today's canvases don't infer. Not picked up.
18. **Charles Goldfarb and Steven Newcomb*** — HyTime (1992). Linking into moments and regions of time-based media. Published as an ISO standard, essentially never implemented. Not picked up.
19. **Steven DeRose** — Overlapping hierarchies (1990s). Markup that lets a text carry several concurrent structures (verse and sentence, page and chapter). Still "the overlap problem"; no mainstream format solves it. Not picked up.
20. **George Furnas** — Generalized fisheye views (1986). Show detail near the focus and context far from it, as a general interface principle. Appears in isolated widgets; never general. Partly absorbed.
21. **Stuart Card, Jock Mackinlay, George Robertson** — Information Visualizer (1991). Cone Trees, the Perspective Wall, 3D information workspaces. Demonstrated at PARC, never a product. Not picked up.
22. **Ramana Rao** — Table Lens (1994) and Inxight. Focus-plus-context tables for thousands of rows. Commercialized, acquired, discontinued. Built and abandoned.
23. **Tim Berners-Lee** — The read-write web (1990) and the Semantic Web (2001). The first browser edited pages; the machine-readable web of linked data was specified in detail. Both largely unadopted as designed. Partly absorbed (Solid is his current attempt).
24. **Bob Stein** — The networked "social book" (2005). Reading as a shared, annotated conversation inside the text. CommentPress proved it; no platform carried it. Partly absorbed.
25. **Danny Hillis** — Knowledge Web (2000). A hypertext map of everything known, with tutoring paths through it; the Aristotle project. Never built. Not picked up.
26. **Gordon Bell and Jim Gemmell** — MyLifeBits (2001). Record everything you read, hear, and see; search it later. Research project, wound down; screen-recording tools revived the surface. Partly absorbed.
27. **David Gelernter*** — Lifestreams (1996). Replace files with one time-ordered stream. Partly absorbed.
28. **Ben Shneiderman** — Genex (1998). A framework and tool suite for creativity support: collect, relate, create, donate. Framework published, suite never built. Not picked up.
29. **Ward Cunningham** — Federated Wiki (2011). Every reader can fork any page; divergent versions live side by side. Built and maintained, adopted by almost no one. Partly absorbed.

## Communication, collaboration and community

30. **Olaf Helmer and Norman Dalkey** — The Delphi method (1950s, RAND). Anonymous, iterative expert consensus. Widely used on paper; rarely built into software. Partly absorbed.
31. **J.C.R. Licklider and Robert Taylor*** — OLIVER (1968). A personal agent that handles routine correspondence. Actively pursued.
32. **Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores*** — The Coordinator (1986). Communication structured as requests and promises. Failed commercially; never retried. Not picked up.
33. **Murray Turoff and Roxanne Hiltz*** — EIES and computerized Delphi (1971). Structured group deliberation. Not picked up.
34. **Jacques Vallée** — FORUM and PLANET (1973). Early computer conferencing at the Institute for the Future, with structured discussion roles and anonymity options. Ideas dispersed into email and forums. Partly absorbed.
35. **Lee Felsenstein, Efrem Lipkin, Mark Szpakowski*** — Community Memory (1973). Public terminals, community-owned. Closed 1992. Built and abandoned.
36. **Donald Bitzer** — PLATO (1960) and its community (Notes, TERM-talk, 1973). Touch screens, plasma displays, forums and chat, decades early, on a mainframe few could reach. The system survived; the community model was rebuilt from scratch by others. Built and abandoned.
37. **Mark Stefik** — Colab (1987). A meeting room where everyone edits the same shared display ("what you see is what I see"). Influenced groupware; the room never shipped. Partly absorbed.
38. **General Magic*** — Telescript and Magic Cap (1994). Mobile code agents. Company died. Partly absorbed.
39. **Bill Atkinson** — Networked HyperCard (1987). By his own account, HyperCard with links across machines would have been the web; he "missed" it. Apple let HyperCard die. Built and abandoned.
40. **Richard Bolt** — Put-That-There (1980). Speech plus pointing as one command. Demonstrated at MIT; voice assistants got the speech and dropped the pointing. Partly absorbed.
41. **Mark Weiser** — Calm technology (1995). Ubiquitous computing that stays in the periphery until needed. Ubiquity arrived; calm did not. Partly absorbed.
42. **Michael Dertouzos** — The Information Marketplace (1991) and Project Oxygen (1999). Human-centered, pervasive computing that serves people rather than the reverse. Research program; ended with his death. Partly absorbed.
43. **David Chaum** — ecash (1990). Anonymous digital cash with cryptographic privacy. DigiCash went bankrupt in 1998; cryptocurrencies took the "digital" and mostly dropped the "anonymous." Partly absorbed.
44. **Amitai Etzioni** — MINERVA (1972). Electronic town halls letting thousands deliberate and vote by phone and TV. Pilot experiments only. Not picked up.
45. **Alvin Toffler** — Anticipatory democracy (1970). Citizen assemblies that plan futures with the help of information technology. Citizen assemblies now exist; the technology layer was never built. Partly absorbed.

## Education

46. **B.F. Skinner** — Teaching machines (1954). Self-paced, immediate-feedback instruction devices. Built, sold, and abandoned as behaviorism fell from favor; the idea returned as apps. Built and abandoned.
47. **Gordon Pask*** — Conversation Theory and adaptive teaching machines (1956–1976). Learning as negotiated dialogue over a concept map. Not picked up.
48. **Patrick Suppes** — Computer-assisted instruction (1966). Individualized drill and tutoring at scale; he predicted "the personal services of a tutor as well-informed as Aristotle." Built at Stanford, commercialized narrowly. Partly absorbed.
49. **Seymour Papert*** — The Knowledge Machine (1993). A machine a child explores by asking. Partly absorbed (closed AI tutors).
50. **Alan Kay*** — Dynabook (1972) and STEPS (2006). The education was removed from the Dynabook; STEPS was ignored. Partly absorbed.
51. **Andrea diSessa** — Boxer (1985). A "computational medium" where everything on screen is a visible, editable box, meant to make programming a literacy like writing. Used in research classrooms, never spread. Not picked up.
52. **Roger Schank** — Goal-based scenarios and ASK systems (1990s). Learning by doing inside simulated situations, with experts' stories retrievable at the moment of need. Built at Northwestern's ILS, faded with funding. Built and abandoned.
53. **Ivan Illich*** — Learning webs (1971). Peer matching for learners. Not picked up.
54. **Neal Stephenson** — The Young Lady's Illustrated Primer (1995, fiction). An adaptive book that raises a child. Now the explicit goal of several AI-tutor startups. Actively pursued.

## Civic life, governance and economics

55. **Patrick Geddes** — The Outlook Tower (1892). A civic observatory where citizens see their own city's data, from the region down to the street, and use it to plan. One tower in Edinburgh; the civic-data idea waited a century. Partly absorbed.
56. **Stafford Beer*** — Cybersyn and the Viable System Model (1971). Not picked up.
57. **Jay Forrester*** — Urban Dynamics (1969). Public policy simulation for cities. Partly absorbed (climate took the form).
58. **Donella Meadows** — World3 and citizen-facing system models (1972). She wanted the models in the public's hands, not just in reports. Models exist; the public tools mostly don't. Partly absorbed.
59. **Buckminster Fuller*** — World Game (1961) and the Dymaxion House (1930). A public resource simulation; a mass-produced house. Neither built at scale. Not picked up.
60. **Robin Hanson*** — Futarchy (2000). Actively pursued (DAOs).
61. **Eric Posner and Glen Weyl** — Quadratic voting (2014). Buy votes at a quadratic price to express intensity of preference. Trialed in Colorado's legislature and by Gitcoin. Actively pursued.
62. **Jaron Lanier and Glen Weyl*** — Mediators of Individual Data (2018). Partly absorbed (data unions).
63. **Robert Propst** — Action Office (1964). A flexible, human-centered workspace. Adopted as the cubicle, which he spent the rest of his life disowning. Partly absorbed, badly.

## Design, architecture and physical space

64. **Cedric Price*** — Fun Palace (1961) and Generator (1976). A reconfigurable building; a building that proposes its own changes. Neither built. Not picked up.
65. **Yona Friedman*** — Flatwriter (1967). Residents design their own dwellings with instant feasibility. Not picked up.
66. **Christopher Alexander** — The Oregon Experiment (1975). A campus planned continuously by its users through pattern languages and small, incremental projects. Adopted on paper at the University of Oregon, abandoned in practice. Built and abandoned.
67. **Kevin Lynch** — Imageability (1960). Tools for reading and designing cities from residents' mental maps. Method survives in research; no working tool. Partly absorbed.
68. **Archigram (Peter Cook)** — Plug-In City (1964). A megastructure into which standardized units plug and unplug. Never built. Not picked up.
69. **Constant Nieuwenhuys** — New Babylon (1959–1974). A city for a post-work society, continuously rebuilt by its inhabitants. Models and drawings only. Not picked up.
70. **Nicholas Negroponte*** — The Architecture Machine (1970) and Dataland (1976). A design partner; a spatial information landscape. Partly absorbed (canvases); the partner never arrived.
71. **Enzo Mari** — Autoprogettazione (1974). Furniture designs published free for anyone to build with planks and nails; a precursor of open source hardware. Book stayed a book. Partly absorbed.
72. **Bret Victor*** — Drawing Dynamic Visualizations (2013) and Dynamicland (2018). Partly absorbed.
73. **Brenda Laurel** — Computers as Theatre (1991) and Placeholder (1993). Interaction designed as drama, with agency and catharsis; a VR world built on that theory. Theory cited, practice thin. Partly absorbed.
74. **Myron Krueger** — Videoplace (1974). Full-body, camera-based "artificial reality" without headsets. Kinect reached part of it thirty years later. Partly absorbed.
75. **Morton Heilig** — Sensorama and the Telesphere Mask (1957–1962). Multi-sensory immersive cinema and the first head-mounted display. Prototypes only; no funding. Built late (VR).

## Health

76. **Lawrence Weed*** — Problem-Knowledge Couplers (1968). Not picked up.
77. **Warner Slack*** — Patient-computer dialogue (1966). Partly absorbed.
78. **Morris Collen** — Automated multiphasic health screening (1964). Routine, computer-driven preventive screening for whole populations at Kaiser. Ran for years, then dismantled as reimbursement changed. Built and abandoned.
79. **Homer Warner** — HELP (1967). Hospital-wide decision support that watches the record and alerts clinicians. Ran at LDS Hospital for decades; the ambitious version never generalized. Partly absorbed.

## Programming, machines and systems

80. **Charles Babbage** — Analytical Engine (1837). General-purpose mechanical computer. Never completed; a working Difference Engine No. 2 was built in 1991. Built late.
81. **Charles Sanders Peirce** — Electrical logic circuits (1886). In a letter, described how switches could perform Boolean logic, fifty years before Shannon. No one built it. Built late.
82. **Konrad Zuse*** — Plankalkül (1945) and *Rechnender Raum* (1969). The first high-level language, implemented in 2000; a cellular-automaton model of physics, unbuilt. Built late.
83. **Alan Turing** — The child machine (1950). Educate a simple machine rather than program an adult one. Proposed in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence"; became the mainstream of AI seventy years later. Built late.
84. **John von Neumann** — The kinematic self-replicating machine (1948). A physical machine that builds copies of itself from parts. Only the cellular-automaton version was ever run. Not picked up.
85. **Heinz von Foerster** — Biological Computer Laboratory (1958–1974). Self-organizing, adaptive computing modeled on living systems. Defunded; ideas scattered into neural networks and cybernetics. Built and abandoned.
86. **Ivan Sutherland*** — Sketchpad constraints (1963). Partly absorbed.
87. **Warren Teitelman** — DWIM and the programmer's assistant (1966–1978). An environment that corrects your errors, remembers what you did, and can undo it; Interlisp. Modern IDEs took fragments. Partly absorbed.
88. **Doug McIlroy** — Mass-produced software components (1968). A catalog of standard, interchangeable parts for software. Package registries realized it fifty years later. Built late.
89. **John Backus** — FP, function-level programming (1977). Programs built by combining functions, with no variables, as an escape from the "von Neumann bottleneck." Influenced functional languages; FP itself never used. Partly absorbed.
90. **Dan Ingalls** — Fabrik (1988). Visual programming by wiring components, in Smalltalk. Prototype at Apple, shelved. Not picked up.
91. **Niklaus Wirth** — Oberon (1987). An entire operating system and language in a few thousand lines, with text as the interface. Built, used at ETH, ignored elsewhere. Built and abandoned.
92. **Rob Pike, Ken Thompson and Bell Labs** — Plan 9 (1992). Everything is a file, including the network, with per-process namespaces. Built, open sourced, never adopted. Built and abandoned.
93. **Jef Raskin*** — The Humane Interface (2000). Not picked up.
94. **Jaron Lanier** — Phenotropic programming (2003). Software modules that recognize each other's behavior instead of relying on rigid protocols. Never implemented. Not picked up.
95. **Douglas Lenat** — Cyc (1984). Hand-encode common sense so machines can reason with it. Built over forty years; never adopted, then overtaken. Built and abandoned.
96. **Marvin Minsky** — The Society of Mind as a software architecture (1986). Intelligence from many simple agents. Influential as a book, never built as designed. Not picked up.
97. **Chris Crawford*** — Storytron (1997–2018). Actively pursued (AI fiction), in a different form.

## Elsewhere

98. **Nikola Tesla** — Wardenclyffe and wireless power (1901). Global wireless transmission of energy and information. Funding withdrawn; tower demolished 1917. Partly absorbed (wireless information, not power).
99. **Norbert Wiener** — The hearing glove (1949). Convert speech to tactile patterns on the hand for deaf users. Prototyped at MIT, dropped. Not picked up.
100. **Michael Nielsen*** — Networked open science (2011). Actively pursued.

## Notes for the catalog

- The people most often missing from lists like this are women and non-Western thinkers, partly because their proposals were less often written up as named systems. Cathy Marshall, Wendy Hall, Roxanne Hiltz, Donella Meadows, Brenda Laurel, Marie Neurath, S.R. Ranganathan, Paul Otlet and Yona Friedman are here; more should be found before the catalog is called complete.
- "Built and abandoned" entries are usually the best candidates for revival: the design was proven, and the reason it stopped was money, platform, or timing rather than the idea.
- "Built late" entries are the argument for the whole project. Peirce, Babbage, Zuse, McIlroy and Turing each waited between thirty and one hundred fifty years.
